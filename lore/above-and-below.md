# Above and Below

Edravoss rises in walls of stone. Most settlements stand in sheltered mountain
basins, where streams feed fields and pasture below the exposed heights. A
city's hearths may be crowded together, but its livelihood spreads across the
basin in farms, gardens, mills and grazing ground.

## The heights

Magic gathers with height. Its force follows elevation rather than the depth
of rock overhead. A passage inside a high ridge can be as troubled as the
slope outside it. Low ground beneath an immense mountain is less exposed to
that force, although it has dangers of its own.

There is no single height above which every journey fails. The strength varies
between ridges and over time. Travellers recognise stretches where weather
turns violent and distances become unreliable. They can learn the signs
without knowing why the heights behave this way.

Surface passes remain possible. A messenger with little to carry may attempt
one when delay below would be worse. Moving a season's grain through the same
pass is another undertaking. Low surface roads serve farms within a basin and
occasionally join neighbouring pockets of land; they do not form a dependable
route across the enclosing ranges.

## Living in a basin

Most basins grow much of their ordinary food. Households dry and preserve what
they can, and settlements keep grain against winter and missed caravans.
Reserves differ with the harvest, the size of the population and who controls
the stores. A full granary does not mean that every household can draw from it.

Trade supplies what local ground cannot provide in sufficient quantity. One
basin may need salt or lamp oil, another iron, seed or additional grain. A
poor harvest can turn a useful food road into an urgent dependency. When a
route closes, people draw down stores, ration, seek other suppliers or leave.
A settlement cut off for years must find a lasting way to support itself;
some shrink, and some are abandoned.

These needs are visible in the places people build. Dry stores stand above
flood-prone ground. Channels take water to fields and mills. Garden walls and
covered yards give shelter from the wind. Under the eaves, repaired clothes
dry beside bright scraps of household cloth. People share ovens, argue over
water and make room at the table for someone who has come home late.

## The Below

The Below is an underground country of natural chambers, rivers, terraces and
narrow passages. Some chambers have their own distant ridges and branching
valleys. Elsewhere two loaded animals cannot pass abreast.

People made roads through this country. They widened constrictions, cut steps,
built retaining walls and linked natural passages with short excavated ways.
Much of that work survives in fragments. An old road can end at a washout and
resume on the far side of a rough natural shelf. The shape of a cavern is not
evidence that someone built it.

The stone does not rearrange itself. Floods close lower ways and expose or
isolate higher ones; silt changes riverbanks and hides footing. Collapses can
destroy a connection permanently. A broad chamber or a high resting shelf may
remain dependable for generations while the crossings beyond it change after
each flood. Rain and thaw above can alter water below, sometimes after a delay.
Conditions at one entrance cannot tell a traveller the state of the whole road.

## Keeping a road open

Caravans need fodder, food, dry loads and light. They cannot count on grazing
below. Hauliers carry feed for their animals as well as their own provisions,
and every extra day uses carrying capacity intended for trade. Lamps reveal
nearby work and footing. Beyond them, much of the country remains unseen.

On regularly used routes, supplied waystations shorten the load each caravan
must carry. Their keepers exchange shelter and stores for payment or goods.
Supplies arrive from the basins; a station cut off from replenishment may have
to close. Returning hauliers carry news of its stores as carefully as news of
a crossing. A lamp on a distant shelf is no promise of a meal.

Scouts check uncertain stretches before the loaded animals follow. Where the
road narrows, loads may be divided and carried through by hand. River travel
helps on suitable reaches, but falls, currents and broken connections prevent
boats from serving the whole network. Large bodies of travellers, including
armies, need supply arrangements and often move in separated groups. A road
that admits a small party may be unable to sustain them.

Sheltered stopping places and changing connections give journeys their rhythm.
People wait, compare accounts, repair gear and decide whether they have enough
left to continue. Turning back while they still can is part of knowing the road.
