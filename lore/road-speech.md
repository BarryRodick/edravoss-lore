# Road Speech and the Cut

The settlements of Edravoss were not founded by people scattering across an
already divided world. The world divided around them.

Before the Long Breaking, roads beneath the mountains connected basins,
waystations and markets. Spoken languages varied, but builders, hauliers and
travellers shared a small operational vocabulary for the things that mattered
underground: road, water, open, closed, above, below, enter and return.

As floods, collapses, failed works and sealed gates divided the network,
settlements became isolated. Hearth Speech changed with each community. Road
Speech survived because changing a recognised warning could kill somebody.

Road Speech is deliberately brief. It is made to be shouted through water and
echoes, understood by strangers and reduced to marks when there is no time for
conversation.

Its written register is called the Cut. The Cut favours quick, angular marks
that can be scratched, pecked, painted or carved into stone. It is not a full
written language. It records practical facts and leaves much of their meaning
to those who still know the road.

Typical statements include:

> WATER RISE.  
> LOWER WAY LOST.  
> FIVE ENTER. THREE RETURN.  
> CITY BEYOND. NO ANSWER.

Most travellers do not carve long accounts. Immediate warnings are scratched
shallowly or drawn in charcoal and ochre. At important waystations, returning
accounts may be compared. Only dangers, losses and changes expected to outlast
the season are given to trained cutters for deeper recording.

The cutter preserves the mark. The recorder decides what the event means.
Neither role is neutral.

Stone remembers slowly. Floodwater erodes shallow cuts, silt fills grooves,
repairs remove old accounts and collapses bury entire walls. Names can be
hammered away. Poor settlements and lost expeditions may never enter a formal
record at all.

The walls do not preserve one history. They preserve the argument.

## Names on the road

A hearthname belongs to family, place and the speech of home. Travellers often
adopt a short roadname below: one or two syllables, easy to shout and distinct
from the names of their companions. A simple personal mark may stand beside it.

Roadnames and marks survive more often than hearthnames. A wall may remember
what someone did long after their home, family and full name have been lost.

Above ground, people have names. Below ground, they have signals. History
remembers the signal.

At old waystones, one exchange is spoken more fully than it is cut:

> The road is broken.  
> Still, we cross.
