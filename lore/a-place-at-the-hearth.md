# A place at the hearth

Before a traveller leaves a basin, somebody must take over the work they will
miss. The animals still need feeding. Water must reach the garden, and a roof
that leaked last winter will leak again. Departure can begin with an argument
over who will mend it. By morning, the same people may be cutting food for the
journey and finding a better piece of cloth to wrap it in.

A household rarely pays for a journey in goods alone. A sister lends her sound
boots and spends the next weeks in patched ones. Neighbours lend a hand at
harvest because they expect the returning load to include salt for them. In a
crowded home, one person's absence gives others room to sleep, even as it leaves
too much work. People can want someone safely back and enjoy the space they
have left behind. They do not always admit both at the table.

The traveller's pack may hold small things that have nothing to do with the
caravan's cargo. Someone has a tool to return to a cousin across the range.
Someone wants a length of cloth delivered, with instructions that it is for
the child who will have grown since the last visit. People send spoken messages
too. They repeat them while straps are tightened, with corrections called from
the doorway, then entrust them to a person who already has much to remember.
After a long delay, a traveller may arrive with
a gift too small for its intended wearer and news the household has known for
months.

Roadnames help companions hear one another below. At home, the person behind
that short call has a hearthname and perhaps a childhood shortening that the
family still uses. A returning traveller can be
proud of a roadname that nobody at the shared oven has heard. Another is glad
to put it aside. If a stranger brings word of someone known only by their
roadname, the household may need to hear where they came from, who travelled
with them, or what they carried before it recognises its own.

News of an absence arrives unevenly. A companion saw someone alive at a station.
A later caravan found that station closed. Neither account tells the family
where the missing traveller is now. Those carrying bad news may have to repeat
the few things they witnessed through a whole evening, as relatives return
from work and ask again. The person who saw a traveller die brings different
news from one who lost sight of them. Some families have only that last sighting
to go on, long after others have begun to speak of the missing person as dead.

Meanwhile, winter comes. Boots left by the door pass to someone who needs them.
Another person takes over the workbench. Some households keep a missing
person's belongings together; others use them and save one small thing. A
quarrel over lending out a coat may be a quarrel over whether its owner is
ever coming home. Practical decisions do not settle that question, however
much the family wishes they would.

Return can be joyful and awkward in the same hour. The place where a traveller
slept may hold a child's bed now. The child they remember may have become the
person who decides when the animals go to pasture. Someone else has paid a
debt, repaired the roof and grown used to making decisions without them. Even
a welcome guest needs feeding until there is work for another pair of hands.
Old affection can survive an argument over who is to have the boots.

Where caravans arrive often, households have more practice at departures and
adjustments. In a basin long out of contact, a return may bring unfamiliar
words to the supper table. People listen, laugh at a changed accent, or ask
the traveller to say something as they used to. Customs differ between homes
as well as basins. In one, everyone stays awake to hear the journey. In another,
the traveller eats while the family finishes its work, and the questions wait
until morning.
