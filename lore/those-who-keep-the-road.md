# Those who keep the road

A road can survive on a map long after the last person paid to mend it has
gone. Below, travellers look for smaller assurances. Silt has been shovelled
off the steps. A loose stone has been bedded again. Someone has carried timber
down from daylight to replace a rail beside the drop.

Such work seldom belongs to everyone who benefits from it. A farming basin
may maintain the descent as far as its first loading shelf. Beyond that, a
haulier pays porters to clear a pinch in the road, and a waystation keeper
patches the approach that brings customers to the door. Between their stretches
lie places each hopes the other will repair.

## Paying for the next crossing

Where trade is regular, neighbours make agreements about the work. One
settlement sends stoneworkers after the floods. Another supplies their food
and replaces broken tools. At some crossings, passing loads pay a toll towards
repairs. Elsewhere, the people who use a road owe days of labour, and a
household with nobody fit to go must find someone to take its share.

These arrangements belong to particular roads. An agreement remembered in one
basin may be disputed in the next, especially after years without contact.
Returning trade can bring an old demand for payment along with the first load
of salt. The people now living beside a crossing may have kept it sound
without help from those claiming the right to collect there.

Repair crews carry their own provisions and light. A washed-out wall can need
more hands than the nearby station can feed. Work waits while someone finds
grain, or the crossing opens only wide enough for a person carrying a bundle.
News that a road has reopened must say what can pass. The pack animals may
still be waiting above.

## A gate and its keeper

Where a narrow approach can be barred, a few people can control a much longer
road. A gate may protect dry stores, keep animals away from a damaged bridge
or give workers room to mend the descent. It can also hold a caravan until its
owners accept a higher charge.

Authority varies. Some keepers answer to the settlement that supplies them.
Others work for the households or hauliers who paid for the station. An
isolated keeper can make decisions that nobody at home will hear about for
weeks. Travellers remember who let them shelter during a flood and who charged
for a place on the last dry floor.

Charges can be challenged at the next market or through those who supply the
gate. Sometimes hauliers arrange another route. Sometimes there is no other
route, and the load is perishable, and the keeper knows it. A basin short of
grain has little time to settle an argument about an old right of passage.

The keepers have their own dependencies. Their lamps, food and replacement
timber come along the roads they control. A gate that takes so much that trade
stops will eventually have hungry people on both sides.

## When the work stops

Closing a road leaves obligations behind. A crew may still be beyond the
crossing. Someone has paid for fodder stored at the next station. At home,
families are expecting wages for work that can no longer be reached.

On a maintained route, keepers try to send word in both directions before
shutting a station. They may have to carry the warning back themselves. There
are abandoned doors with no final account beside them. A broken approach can
prevent anyone reaching the wall where a warning would have been useful.

Maintenance also puts people in charge of what survives. Clearing a drain
can save the lower road and scour shallow marks from a wall downstream.
Replacing unsafe masonry can remove an account of the last failure. Where
there is time, a cutter copies an important mark onto sound stone. The copy
still depends on what the cutter could read and what the person paying for
the work thought worth keeping.

## At the station table

Travellers arriving at a working station bring several kinds of news. A
crossing is dry. The rail beyond it is loose. A household in the upper basin
has lamp oil to sell. The keeper needs all of it before deciding how many
people can be fed and which repairs can be attempted.

The table also gathers people whose interests differ. The owner of a waiting
load wants the road declared open. The porter who crossed that morning knows
the ledge is crumbling. Other travellers listen for where and when each person
went, and whether a loaded animal could follow. Speaking against the person
who pays your wages can cost you your next journey.

A useful keeper remembers who has actually seen the road. The names matter
again when someone comes through with a different account.
