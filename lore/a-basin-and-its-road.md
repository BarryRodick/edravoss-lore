# A basin and its road

This example follows an unnamed farming basin and a supply journey. Its
distances and stores belong to the example, not to every road in Edravoss.

## Home

The basin grows grain and keeps livestock. Most years its harvest and winter
stores feed the settlement. Salt, iron and some lamp oil arrive through the
Below. After a poor harvest, the settlement also buys grain from a neighbour.
By the time this caravan leaves, its remaining food will last six weeks at
the current ration.

From the storehouse yard, the travellers can see water moving through the
fields. Someone is baking in a shared oven. Under a covered gallery, a child
holds up a mended red sleeve while its owner finishes loading. The hauliers
take empty grain sacks and tools to exchange. Bringing grain home will leave
less room for other goods.

## The entrance

The usual journey to the neighbouring basin takes four days each way, with a
supplied waystation after the second day. The caravan carries food, animal
feed and lamp fuel for six days between replenishments. It expects
to buy supplies at the station and again at its destination. That margin
allows a short delay, not a search of unknown length.

At the entrance, dry ground gives way to worn steps and then a natural slope.
Porters divide bundles that would catch on the narrow descent. A returning
haulier reports that the station was stocked two days earlier. The guide
asks about the crossing as well. A report about food is not a report about
the road that leads to it.

## The crossing

On the second day, the caravan reaches a broad shelf above the river. An old
retaining wall ends in water. Beyond it, the lower crossing has drowned. The
resting shelf is sound; the connection to the waystation is uncertain.

The animals wait together on dry ground. Two scouts leave with lamps to test
a higher ledge. Beside the old crossing, fresh charcoal points towards a water
level scratched on the stone. A deeper warning survives underneath, partly
cancelled by another hand. The guide compares both with the river before
trusting either.

After two days' travel, the caravan has four days of provisions left. Waiting
one day for the scouts would still leave time to return to the entrance with
a day's margin, if the way behind stays open. Dividing the grain loads later
may make the higher ledge usable. If it does not, the caravan must turn back
and report while the settlement still has time to arrange another attempt.

Six weeks of food at home and a few days of feed below are different clocks.
The people making the journey have to answer to both.
